Tax when you get a pension. Income Tax on payments from pensions, tax-free allowances, how you pay tax on pensions. You’ll usually pay tax on income from your pensions. This includes your State Pension. The amount of tax you pay depends on the total income you receive during the tax year. Types of pensions There are different types of pensions that you may pay tax on, for example: the basic State Pension or the new State Pension Additional State Pension a private pension such as a workplace or personal pension Tax if you inherit a pension Different rules apply if you inherit a State pension or a private pension . You will not usually pay any tax if your total annual income adds up to less than your Personal Allowance - this is usually £12,570. Lump sums from your pension You can usually take up to 25% of the amount built up in any pension as a tax-free lump sum. The most you can take is £268,275. If you hold a protected allowance, this may increase the amount of tax-free lump sums you can take from your pensions. The tax-free lump sum does not affect your Personal Allowance. Tax is taken off the remaining amount before you get it. Example Your whole pension is worth £60,000. You take £15,000 as a tax-free lump sum. Your pension provider will then take off the tax from the remaining £45,000. When you can take your pension depends on your pension scheme’s rules. It’s usually 55 at the earliest. You might have to pay Income Tax at a higher rate if you take a large amount from your pension. You could also owe extra tax at the end of the tax year. If you take a small pot lump sum from a pension that you’ve not already accessed, 25% of it is tax-free. You can usually get: up to 3 small pot lump sums worth up to £10,000 each from different personal pensions unlimited small pot lump sums from different workplace pensions If your private pensions are worth £30,000 or less If all your private pensions are worth £30,000 or less, you may be able to take the whole amount from certain types of pensions as a lump sum. This is called a ‘trivial commutation’ lump sum. You can usually do this if the pension is a defined benefit pension. If you take the lump sum from a pension that you’ve not already accessed, 25% of it is tax-free. If you’ve already accessed the pension, the whole lump sum is taxable. How your State Pension is taxed. Find out how tax on your State Pension is worked out when you have other income. Your State Pension is taxable income, but tax is not taken off before you get it. You’ll only pay tax if your total taxable income , including your State Pension, is higher than your personal allowances. How we work out your taxable State Pension The State Pension rates usually change in April. If you started receiving your State Pension on or after 6 April 2010, we base a full year’s State Pension on: one week at your weekly rate before the amount changes in April 51 weeks at your weekly rate after the amount changes in April To work out your taxable State Pension amount, we use the amounts you were entitled to get over the tax year, rather than the payments you actually received. These amounts are shown on your State Pension letter from the Department for Work and Pensions ( DWP ). Example Your weekly State Pension is: £160 per week (before the amount changes in April) £170 per week (after the amount changes in April) To calculate your taxable State Pension amount for the year we use: £160 × 1 week = £160 £170 × 51 weeks = £8,670 We add these together to get your total taxable State Pension = £8,830. If you start to receive your State Pension part way through the year we tax you on the number of weeks you receive payments for. Example Your State Pension entitlement started on 4 January 2026 at £170 per week. The first payment you received was on 4 March 2026. This payment included the pension amounts due from 4 January 2026 to 4 March 2026. To calculate your taxable State Pension amount we work out the number of weeks left in the tax year from your entitlement start date and multiply this by your weekly State Pension amount. £170 × 13 weeks = £2,210 total taxable State Pension. We’ll use your total taxable State Pension figure when working out your Income Tax. You’ll also need to include this figure in your Self Assessment tax return if you submit one. How we work out if you owe tax This is a simple explanation of how we work out your tax. It can be more complicated, depending on your income and circumstances. We add your taxable State Pension to your other taxable income. This is your total taxable income for the year. We then take off any allowances you’re entitled to and tax any remaining income using the Income Tax rates that apply to you. Find out more information about Income Tax rates and Personal Allowances . How you’ll pay tax If you have other income from employment or pensions We’ll usually change your tax code so the tax due is collected from your wages or other pensions. If we’re unable to collect the tax due through your tax code, we may send you a Simple Assessment tax calculation after the end of the tax year. If you submit Self Assessment tax returns You’ll need to include your annual State Pension entitlement amount on your tax return. If you use Making Tax Digital for Income Tax software When you submit your tax return you must check that your State Pension entitlement amount is included.
